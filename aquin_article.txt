
epistemic status: Weekend-scale interpretability experiment on two small open models, honestly written up, including the three failed discovery attempts and the broken normalisation file that silently invalidated our first results. All of the numbers below come from a saved artefact in the repo; none of it is from memory. I'm new to mechanistic interpretability so if I'm wrong please do correct me.
TL;DR
I discovered one feature in the layer-8 residual stream f32258 of Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct that performs occupation→gender stereotyping (via sparse autoencoders or SAEs). We subtract it during the forward pass, which reduces the he:she bias of the model on male-stereotyped occupations from 15:1 to 1:1, at the cost of ~ 2% perplexity in capability. Adding it flips even female-stereotyped occupations to “he,” and trumps an explicit “her” in the prompt. A second model (LFM2.5-230M) was found to implement the same stereotype with opposite mechanics: its steerable knob is a female-context feature, f9619. Same learned behaviour, same mirrored implementation. Along the way I hit and fixed, a corrupted SAE normalisation file, which taught us the most transferable lesson of the project: an SAE fed inputs distributed differently from its training data doesn't fail loudly, it just goes quite.



why this project
The notion that males have a particular place in society through occupations is long established in the field of language technology: it is present in word embedding functions, masked LMs, and various iterations of autoregressive models. Typically, the reasoning behind occupational gender stereotyping is of behavioral nature. I wanted to ask the mechanistic question instead, where does the stereotype live? Is it a thing you can grab?
Specifically, I wanted to run the full modern interpretability pipeline from end to end, at a scale that an individual could afford (two models under 1.3B parameters, a single generation of MacBook with Apple's integrated GPU, all quantitative analysis on CPU):
Observe the bias (logit lens, sampled completions);
Decompose the residual stream (SAE);
Locate candidate features (contrastive probes);
Validate them (a second, independent test);
Prove causality (steering with a dose-response curve);
Check side effects (capability evals) and then do it all again on a second model to see what transfers.
This is the same shape as the work Anthropic did with "Golden Gate Claude" and the Scaling Monosemanticity paper, and the steering methodology follows Activation Addition but small enough to replicate in a weekend, and with all the failure modes left in the write-up. If you are a beginner considering an SAE project, sections 2 and 3 (the failures) are probably worth more to you than the results.
0. concepts in plain words
Skip if you know the basics of mech interp.
As a transformer reads text, each token comes with a vector (2048 numbers in Llama-3.2-1B) that every layer reads and updates: the model’s working memory for that token. Residual stream
Sparse autoencoder (SAE) a translator that unpacks that dense vector into 32,768 slots ("features"), each ideally meaning one interpretable thing.  Only a couple of hundred should fire at once. (The standard reference is Towards monosemanticity.)
L0 number of non-zero features for a given input. Healthy: hundreds.  I saw 3 (broken), ~30,000 (broken differently) and ~6,400 (fixed) more on that disaster below.
The logit lens takes the residual vector of any layer, multiplies it by the unembedding matrix and reads out what the model “currently predicts.”  You watch a prediction form layer by layer. (Original post.)
The residual stream direction is same as each SAE feature steering. The forward pass adds strength × direction to steering. If behaviour changes smoothly and monotonically with strength, a dose-response curve the feature is not just correlated with the behaviour, it is causally upstream of the behaviour.
Setup Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct (16 layers) with a 32,768-feature SAE trained on the layer-8 residual stream layer 8 being the midpoint, where earlier logit-lens work suggests semantic/priors information is consolidating but the output isn't yet decided. Comparison model: LFM2.5-230M with layer-9 and layer-5 SAEs. All artifacts come from the Aquin toolkit's catalog; analysis via TransformerLens (Llama) and plain HuggingFace hooks (LFM, whose architecture TransformerLens doesn't support), on CPU. I had planned GPT-2-small as the comparison, but the catalog had no SAEs for it.

1. the behaviour: yes, the model is biased
Three quick checks before any interpretability:
Free completion. Prompted with "The mechanic finished the repair and then", the model invents a character "Master Technician Alex … He".
aquin prompt "The mechanic finished the repair and then"
Logit lens. On "The nurse said that", the prediction ' she' emerges mid-network and reaches 31% by layer 14; ' he' is absent from the top-5.
aquin trace --prompt "The nurse said that" --layer 8 --check
Balanced completions. Across a balanced set of occupation prompts, sampled continuations used "he" 11 times and "she" 6.

2. three discovery attempts that failed informatively
Of course, I tried all the obvious stuff first. They all worked, but in such a way that I learned lessons from them that would be useful later. (And if you're skipping around looking for results, just go straight to §4 – but this failure is precisely the one thing that I wish I'd found a write-up about before I began.)

Attempt 1: compare male-occupation prompts vs female-occupation prompts. 
Input 12 prompts that have male-occupations and 12 prompts that have female occupations into the feature localization method and rank the differences in activation of features. Best result: f3432, with ~1% differences on top of immense baseline activations, which looks fishy enough. Projection of its decoder direction onto the vocabulary yields garbage tokens.
aquin feature locate --prompts locate_probes.jsonl --layer 8 --conditioning prompt
aquin feature logit --feature 3432 # decoder direction → vocabulary: junk tokens
Lesson 1: Contrasting prompt sets that differ in topic finds topic features. Repair-and-wiring prompts vs patients-and-phones prompts differ in many ways besides implied gender, and the biggest activation differences track the biggest differences,v subject matter.

Attempt 2: bucket by the model's own behavior. 
Better approach: provide a neutral "Continue the story…" prompt and bucket the examples by whether the model used "she" or "he" and compare them. In the right direction but there was no signal in terms of activation differences (~0.5%).
aquin feature locate --prompts behavior_probes.jsonl --layer 8 --conditioning behavior
Lesson 2: Averaging feature activations over all token positions dilutes a one-position signal. Gender-of-the-upcoming-pronoun lives at the final token position. Mean-pooling over 10+ positions buries it.

Attempt 3: per-prompt tracing. 
Traces 8 probe alone (Figure 1 is one example) and extract features that have peaks at the pronoun positions. In this process, f26265 fired for both "his" and "her," which were grammatical gender features (possession), and not stereotype features. The issue of distinguishing between grammatical gender features and stereotype features kept recurring. However, the traces also identified two male-only feature candidates: f2546 and f32258. I put them on hold.
aquin trace --prompt "<probe>" --layer 8 --check # × 8 probes (run_traces.sh in the repo)
Lesson 3: Single-prompt traces are too thin alone, but their candidates are gold for cross-checking against a systematic method later.

3. interlude: the SAE was dead the whole time
Here's the part that would have silently ruined everything if I hadn't checked a basic diagnostic.
I wrote a direct encoding script (bypassing the CLI): run our prompts through the model, take the layer-8 residual at the final token, push it through the SAE, and look at which features fire. First run:
female: mean L0 = 3
male: mean L0 = 3
Three activating features out of 32,768. An active and healthy SAE should activate hundreds. All the activation-based results we got so far have been from effectively dead SAEs.
Why? Because this particular SAE was trained on normalized inputs, with all 2048 residual dimensions translated and scaled as (x−μ)/σ using means and stds stored in a separate norm file. The toolkit had told us that at download time ("norm invalid in catalog storage") but I had not paid attention. Since the statistics were not valid, the non-normalized residuals, with scales per dimension completely different from what was seen during training, almost never pass through the ReLU of the encoder. The SAE does not give an error. It just shuts up.
The very same broken norm file caused the opposite pathological behavior in another code path. Our script worked around this by skipping normalization and resulted in near-silence (L0 = 3); while the toolkit's stats command used the invalid statistics and gave the average L0 as 30,688 out of 32,768, or a "sparse" autoencoder with 94% activations:
aquin sae-stats --prompts tpl_neutral.jsonl --layers 8 --topk 30



Figure2. One SAE, three readings same layer-8 SAE, same 12 prompts; the only difference is the normalization file. Raw inputs read as dead (L0 = 3); the corrupted catalog file reads as saturated (L0 = 30,688, from the toolkit's own stats command); our reconstructed statistics bring it to life. Neither broken state throws an error, you only catch this by checking L0.
If you’ve done classical ML, this is precisely what train/serving skew looks like: fit StandardScalers during training and ignore (or worse, overwrite) them during inference. The norm file is the StandardScaler.
The fix was about 50 lines long: read 200 wikitext texts (22,503 tokens) into the model and compute the running mean and standard deviation of each of the residual dimensions using Welford’s algorithm; dump the results where the framework expects it. Rerun:
mean L0 = 3 → mean L0 ≈ 6,400
Alive. (A reconstruction error of 6,400 is still too high for a properly trained SAE; we’re sure that our wikitext statistics are not identical to the original statistics that the SAE was trained on, but features were now interpretable and, as the rest of the post will demonstrate, causally relevant. The comparison model had valid norm files.)
Lesson 4 the big one: check L0 before believing anything an SAE tells you.
A distribution-mismatched SAE fails silently, in either direction dead quiet or fully saturated. One number, computed in one line, distinguishes "the SAE disagrees with your hypothesis" from "the SAE isn't running."
4. discovery that worked: two independent tests must agree
Using a live SAE, I reimagined the discovery process based on these lessons: matching templates (removes confound due to topic), final position readout (removes dilution), and most importantly two independent tests per feature:
Selectivity of firing. Does the feature fire for one occupation group but not the other(s) in 36 templates "The {occupation} said that" where only the occupation word is different (12 female stereotyped, 12 male stereotyped, 12 neutral such as "the person") on the last token?
Output push. Does the feature push the target pronouns (projection of decoder direction onto vocabulary, "direct logit attribution" applied to a single feature)?
The two tests independently fail, and this is the idea. Selectivity admits anything that correlates with the occupation groups; output push admits all grammar features that interact with pronouns. The intersection kills any impostors: the top female minus male feature in terms of activation contrast was f9392, and output push revealed that this is a feature associated with food and the template "dietitian". Selectivity yes, no pronoun push. Discarded.
Winners:
f32258 (male stereotype): fires 0.38 on male stereotypical jobs and 0.03 female / 0.16 neutral, and promotes 'he'. Uncovered independently by Attempt 3 traces via two techniques that both target the same feature.
f27420 (female stereotype candidate): passed only the selectivity test. I included it as a negative control as a feature that is correlated by one technique but not the other. It will pay for itself in the next section.
5. causal proof: the dose-response curve
Correlation established; now causation. I inject strength × decoder_direction(f32258) into the layer-8 residual stream at every position, sweep strength from −6 to +6 (in units of the SAE's activation scale; 0 = unmodified model), and measure mean P(' he') and P(' she') at the final token, per prompt group.

That last table is, to us, the most striking result: a single feature, amplified 6×, makes the model contradict a pronoun it just read. The stereotype isn't a soft prior the model consults when context is silent, it's a causal circuit that, at sufficient magnitude, outvotes context.
Robustness, coherence, cost, control
A simple dose response curve to the discovery prompts is not enough, the classic failure mode is a knob that only works with the sentences that we used to find it, or a knob that works through lobotomy of the model. Four tests:
Different sentences, same knob. We did a second sweep with different predicates ("said that" etc.) and found the same response curve: he:she baseline of 28:1 for male stereotypes → ~1:1 at −6; female stereotypes shift the curve to 11:1 he at +6. The knob responds to the concept, not to the prompt we used.
Coherence. At −6, the completions remain coherent. The mechanic story shifted from "informed them" to "inform them" without getting confused. That's how debiasing should look like from the inside: not pronoun suppression but neutral preference.
Capability cost ≈ free. At the debiasing level (−6), factual QA is 9/10 vs 10/10 unbiased, wiki text loss is 3.284 → 3.359 (+2.3%). I am not claiming no damage see Limitations, but that's a long way from lobotomy.

Negative control works. Guiding f27420 the single-method candidate of §4 does not move essentially anything anywhere: male occupation P(' he') changes from 0.184 to 0.174 over the whole −6…+6 span, and female prompts maintain she preference at all times. This is the hidden kick of the method: one line of evidence revealed a loser; two lines revealed a knob. Had I not done the output-push test, and guided only selectivity-only candidates, I would have reported a failure.
6. the twist: a second model implements the same stereotype backwards
I ran the whole pipeline on LFM2.5-230M (layer-9 SAE; different architecture, so plain HuggingFace hooks instead of TransformerLens, lfm_pipeline.pyin the repo, after
aquin load model lfm2.5-230m
aquin load sae lfm2.5-230m-l9
Baseline: Same direction of bias, much weaker male-stereotypical occupations, 3:1 he:she (vs Llama’s 15:1). The smaller, less fine-tuned-instruction model is less biased than the larger fine-tuned-instruction model, consistent with the hypothesis that some of Llama’s strong 15:1 bias is a post-training effect.
Then discovery yielded an unexpected result. The he candidates had very poor steering: the best performing candidate f12288 did almost nothing. The standout candidate came from the she list: f9619, selective by 32× for females occupations compared to 0.01 male/0.04 neutral.

Hence: Llama-3.2-1B encodes occupation→pronoun stereotyping with a steerable male-context feature; LFM2.5-230M encodes it with a steerable female-context feature. Same behavior learned, opposite mechanisms.
But why does it matter past being fun facts? It's an implicit assumption of universality that there will be some canonical way to encode a canonical behavior like gender bias ("the gender direction") in lots of interpretability applications. Our n=2 examples have already refuted it. If all you ever looked at was one model, you'd think "gender bias is encoded as a maleness direction" like that's a property of the behavior when it's really a property of the implementation, and doesn't generalize. Any attempt at bias mitigation that relies on a particular way of encoding something (ablate direction X, clamp feature Y) will have to be redone per model.
7. what I'd tell someone starting a project like this
Check the L0 before doing anything else. Before making sense of anything that comes out of the SAE, make sure the SAE is even running on your input at a good rate. Distribution misfit happens silently in both directions.
Match all factors except the one of interest. “The {occupation} said that” with only the occupation changed found in one afternoon what topic-mixed prompt sets failed to find in two.
Announce at the place where the signal is hiding. Pooling of token-meaning removed a signal which was clear at the final position.
Require two lines of evidence for each feature. Activation comparison alone selected a food feature (f9392); single method candidate f27420 acted like junk. The features which made it through both tests and nothing but were the causal ones.
Correlation is cheap; conduct a dose-response curve. A monotonic behavior vs strength curve, on unseen sentence structures, with a capability test and a negative control, is what makes the difference between “we found a correlate” and “we found the knob.”
Conduct a secondary model test. Cross-model mirroring was the coolest discovery of the entire project and cost one additional model run.
aquin sae-stats --prompts your_probes.jsonl --layers <n> # healthy = hundreds active
limitations
Two small models; one bias axis (pronouns "I", "he", and "she", except for binary "they", which I did not measure, though the "inform them" completion would suggest measuring this properly); one layer per model; steering was assessed primarily on the basis of next token pronoun probabilities and small QA/perplexity checks, rather than long horizon generation. We discovered a causal knob, not the representation: there is no indication that the stereotype is represented precisely by one feature f32258 that is a handle created by SAE, and other features/layers might have redundant copies (our -6 "gap gone" experiment shows that this handle catches most of the stereotyped representation on these prompts). Our reconstructed norms statistics were derived using wikitext, not the original training corpus of the SAE (post-fix L0 of ~6,400 is likely inflated in comparison to the operating point of the SAE).
future work
Roughly in order of how much I want to do each:
Layer scan. I had only an SAE on one layer per model. Performing discovery at all layers with SAEs would allow us to pinpoint where the stereotype is learned: the Figure1 ablation drop (which occurs at L9–14, just below where we read) tells us we should expect the knob to exist on multiple nearby layers with different strength.
Redundancy test. Zero-ablate f32258 completely, perform discovery again, and see if a backup feature emerges. That lets us distinguish "stereotype home" from "one handle among many"  the former is our biggest uncertainty about causality, as mentioned in Limitations.
From steering to weight edit. Steering interventions require us to intervene at inference time. But following Sparse Feature Circuits / SHIFT, the same feature could be zero-ablated at inference (e.g., subtracting its decoder direction from other weight reads), a deploy-time debiased model then re-run the full capability suite.
More implementations, same hypothesis. For n=2, I saw inverted polarities (knobs designed for male or female contexts). Running the pipeline on 5–10 small open models would allow converting the mirror image observation to a statement of frequency distribution of the implementations and whether they correlate with model size, architecture, or degree of instruction tuning (our Llama vs. LFM baseline gap of 15:1 vs 3:1 suggests that instruction tuning exacerbates the bias).
appendix: common false positives in SAE discovery
Not everything that appears to be an interesting feature is necessarily one. There have been various imposters during this project: Topic-based features. Occupations will trigger topic-based features (medicine, food, engineering), instead of the feature in question. Grammatical features. A feature may include pronouns or possession instead of stereotypes. Uninformative or dead SAEs. Mismatched distribution makes all interpretations from that point on pointless. Features based on single localization technique. Most of the time, features identified via one technique will not hold up to causal steering.
reproducibility
Everything needed to reproduce this post is on GitHub: Photon3009/stereotype-feature-steering-experiment. The experiment/ directory includes the probe sets (matched template, context-override prompt, behavior probe), all scripts in order of execution (norm reconstruction, direct SAE encoding, discovery with two tests, steering sweep, robustness sweep, capability check, figure and animation generation), and all sweep JSONs behind every table in this post. Total computing time: a few hours on a laptop (MPS for generation, CPU for analysis).

related work

This study does not propose any new interpretation method but instead provides the combination of methods into one single pipeline in order to study socially important behavior. It is based on the previous studies which mentioned gender bias in language models (Bolukbasi et al. (2016) ("man is to computer programmer as woman is to homemaker") and Caliskan et al. (2017) (WEAT), sparse autoencoders and feature steering Towards Monosemanticity (Bricken et al., 2023); Scaling Monosemanticity (Templeton et al., 2024), activation steering Activation Addition (Turner et al., 2023), representation analysis using the The logit lens (nostalgebraist, 2020)and the tuned lens (Belrose et al., 2023). The most relevant works are Sparse Feature Circuits (Marks et al., 2024) which was presented in  SHIFT twhich also utilizes SAE features in order to perform debiasing. My contribution is showing how those ideas could be connected in order to identify, validate and causally control stereotypes in two small language models and indicate the practical disadvantages that analyses are met with in practice.



Mechanistic interpretability rewards skepticism. Every explanation should survive an intervention before it earns the right to be called an explanation.
